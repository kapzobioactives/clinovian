Why Exhibit F matters. The uncertain-diagnosis convention in the FY2026 ICD-10-CM Official Guidelines (Sections II.H and III.C) applies to a diagnosis documented as probable, suspected or likely at the time of discharge. A day-3 progress note saying “likely ATN” does not by itself establish the final coding basis. It is the discharge summary at Exhibit F that documents ATN as a final diagnosis. Where a comparable discharge statement is absent from a real record, the analysis below does not hold and the case should be worked as an unspecified-AKI scenario instead.

Conclusion
Conditional clinical-validation conclusion
Conditional — supports clinical validation review

On the fictional record as supplied, the clinical criteria for acute kidney failure with acute tubular necrosis are met and the condition was evaluated, monitored and treated throughout the stay. The auditor's stated reason for removing the MCC — that the injury was transient and resolved — is a statement about clinical outcome and does not, on its own, answer whether the condition was validly diagnosed, reportable, or correctly classified.

This conclusion holds only if all of the following are true. Each is a client-side verification, not a Clinovian finding:

  1. The 12 March creatinine of 1.1 mg/dL is an acceptable pre-morbid baseline and no intercurrent renal event occurred between that date and admission.
  2. The discharge summary (Exhibit F) is the signed final document and its ATN statement was not subsequently amended.
  3. The reported code is N17.0 rather than N17.9, and that selection is supported by the documentation the coding team relies on.
  4. No CC/MCC exclusion applies between the principal diagnosis as sequenced and N17.0 under the grouper version applicable to the 20 June 2026 discharge.
  5. No other reported secondary diagnosis already supplies an MCC, which would make this dispute immaterial to the DRG.

What this is not. It is not a prediction that the appeal will succeed, not a coding determination, and not a statement that the payer's decision was made in bad faith.

Payer Position
Auditor's Downgrade Rationale

The payer's post-payment audit downgraded DRG 871 to 872 on the following basis:

  • The acute kidney injury was "transient" — creatinine peaked at 3.4 mg/dL on Day 3 but returned to 1.6 mg/dL by discharge.
  • No dialysis was required, and the patient was not transferred to a higher level of care for renal management.
  • The auditor concluded the AKI did not represent a "major" complication because it resolved with conservative management (IV fluids and medication adjustment).

The auditor's rationale conflates clinical resolution with coding classification. These are distinct standards.

Code note — the point the prior appeal got wrong. “AKI is an MCC” is not a correct statement and should not appear in any submission.

  • N17.9 — acute kidney failure, unspecified — is a CC on the FY2026 MS-DRG v43 CC/MCC list.
  • N17.0 — acute kidney failure with tubular necrosis — is an MCC on that list, subject to the CC/MCC exclusion logic applied against the principal diagnosis and to the grouper version in force.
  • The KDIGO stage does not determine which N17 subtype is reported. Subtype selection follows the provider's documentation of the underlying renal lesion — here, the ATN documented at Exhibit F — not the magnitude of the creatinine rise.

The dispute therefore has to be framed around a named code. If the claim reported N17.9, the classification is a CC and the auditor's removal of the MCC may be arithmetically correct even though the clinical picture is severe; the remedy in that situation is a coding review, not an appeal argument about severity. If the claim reported N17.0, the classification question turns on exclusions and grouping, not on whether the injury resolved. The client's coding team must confirm which code was reported before this memo is used.

Rebuttal
Point-by-Point Response to Auditor
Payer Auditor

"The AKI was transient and resolved with conservative management."

Clinovian Response

Reportability of a secondary diagnosis under Guideline III turns on whether the condition required clinical evaluation, therapeutic treatment, diagnostic procedures, an extended stay, or increased nursing care. It does not require persistence to discharge, and it does not set an intervention threshold such as dialysis. On this record the condition was evaluated (Exhibits C, E), treated (Exhibits C, D) and documented as a final diagnosis (Exhibit F). Resolution during the stay does not remove a condition that met those criteria.

Scope note: this answers reportability only. It does not answer which code was reported, whether that code is a CC or an MCC, or what the grouper returns.

Payer Auditor

"No dialysis was required."

Clinovian Response

Correct, and not relevant to the classification question. Renal replacement therapy is one of three alternative KDIGO Stage 3 criteria, so its absence does not exclude Stage 3 when the creatinine criterion is met. More importantly, CC/MCC status is a property of the reported ICD-10-CM code on the FY2026 v43 list — it is not conferred or withheld by whether a particular procedure was performed. The auditor has introduced a procedural threshold that exists in neither framework.

Payer Auditor

"The complication did not rise to the level of 'major.'"

Clinovian Response

The CC/MCC distinction is a code-level classification published by CMS, not a clinical impression formed case by case. The auditor's “not major enough” reasoning has no place in that framework. But the correction runs in both directions: acute kidney injury is not uniformly an MCC. Unspecified acute kidney failure (N17.9) is a CC; acute kidney failure with tubular necrosis (N17.0) is an MCC, subject to exclusions and grouping. The answer depends on the code that was reported against the documentation at Exhibit F, and on the grouper version applicable to the discharge date. This memo supplies the clinical basis for ATN. It does not assert the classification outcome.

CDI / Coding Handoff
Notes for the CDI and Coding Team

This memo provides the clinical reasoning that supports the MCC classification. The following operational notes are for the CDI specialist and coding professional completing the appeal:

  • Confirm which N17 code was reported. This is the first step and it determines whether the dispute is viable at all. N17.9 is a CC; N17.0 is an MCC. Nothing else in this memo can be used until that is established.
  • Verify principal-diagnosis sequencing and the full secondary-diagnosis set. Confirm the sepsis sequencing against the FY2026 guidelines, then check whether any other reported secondary diagnosis already supplies an MCC — if one does, the DRG is unaffected and the dispute is moot.
  • Apply the CC/MCC exclusion logic and the correct grouper version. Exclusions run against the principal diagnosis as sequenced, and the grouper version applicable to the 20 June 2026 discharge governs. Do not rely on a prior-year table.
  • Query only where a genuine ambiguity exists, and query compliantly. On this fictional record the discharge summary (Exhibit F) already documents ATN as a final diagnosis, so no query is indicated. Where a real record documents only unspecified AKI, the ambiguity to resolve is the nature of the renal lesion — not the payment consequence. A compliant query presents the clinical indicators, offers the full range of clinically reasonable options including “unable to determine”, and does not indicate which answer is preferred. A query must never be described, internally or in a submission, as a way to reach a better financial result.
  • Attach the full creatinine trend. The trajectory from baseline through peak to discharge is the single most persuasive piece of evidence. Present it as a table or timeline, not buried in lab reports.
  • Cite the guideline sections by number. Guideline III for secondary-diagnosis reporting; Sections II.H and III.C for the uncertain-diagnosis convention and why the discharge summary rather than the day-3 note carries it. Clinovian supplies the clinical validation; the guideline citation and its application are the coding team's.

This memo complements your CDI workflow — it does not replace it. Clinovian's contribution is the clinical validation: whether the documented diagnosis is supported by the record, and where the auditor's clinical reasoning departs from the applicable frameworks. Code selection, CC/MCC classification, exclusion logic, grouping and the final appeal position are the coding and CDI team's, and this memo should not be filed without that review.

Recommendation
Recommended next step

Refer to coding for validation, then decide. The clinical record supports a valid diagnosis of acute kidney failure with acute tubular necrosis, documented as a final diagnosis at discharge, and the auditor's stated rationale does not address that question. That is the extent of what this memo establishes.

Before anything is filed, the coding team should confirm the reported code, the full secondary-diagnosis set, exclusion logic, and the grouper result for the applicable version. If the reported code is N17.9, the correct response is a coding review rather than a severity appeal. If it is N17.0 and the grouper confirms a DRG change, the clinical material in this memo supports the submission the coding team constructs.

Operational Note
Prevention Note

For future similar cases. The vulnerability in this fictional record is specific and narrow: the nature of the renal lesion appears only as “likely ATN” in a day-3 progress note, and is carried to a final diagnosis solely by the discharge summary. Where a discharge summary is less explicit, the same clinical picture supports only unspecified acute kidney failure — a different code with a different classification.

The practical suggestion for the client's physician and CDI teams is that when the underlying renal lesion is clinically determinable, documenting it by name in the discharge summary removes an ambiguity that would otherwise require a query or leave the encounter coded as unspecified. This is a documentation-completeness observation. It is not a claim that better documentation would have prevented this audit, and it should not be used to imply that a particular denial or downgrade was avoidable.

The Two-Midnight Rule: Why Most Observation Appeals Fail

Clinovian Insights · Level of Care · June 2026
Written and reviewed by
Arun Kasturi, MBBS — physician reviewer with payer-side utilization-management review experience. Not a U.S.-licensed clinician and not a specialty-board diplomate.

Medicare applicability guide

The Two-Midnight Rule is a prospective standard. Most appeals treat it as a retrospective one.

This article is about where the Medicare framework applies. For arguing a status denial under a commercial plan policy — which does not run on CMS authority — see the companion article, Inpatient vs. observation under a commercial policy.

The most common error in this area is treating "the Two-Midnight Rule" as one undifferentiated thing. It is three provisions with different reach, and an appeal has to say which one it is invoking.

Observation-status downgrades are a common denial category, and in this reviewer's payer-side experience a frequently under-argued one. Payers retrospectively reclassify inpatient admissions to observation status after discharge — often months later — arguing that the patient's clinical course did not warrant inpatient-level care.

The appeals that fail almost universally make the same mistake: they argue the patient was sick. The appeals that succeed argue that the admitting physician's expectation was reasonable.

What the rule actually says

CMS's Two-Midnight Rule establishes that inpatient status is appropriate when the admitting physician has a reasonable expectation, at the time of admission, that the patient will require hospital care spanning at least two midnights. The standard is prospective — it evaluates the physician's clinical judgment at the point of admission, not the patient's actual length of stay or final diagnosis.

This distinction matters. A patient admitted with chest pain and troponin elevation, discharged once the workup is negative, does not retroactively become an observation patient — provided the expectation at admission was reasonable on the information then available.

Count the midnights before you argue the expectation

An elapsed-hours figure does not tell you how many midnights were crossed. A 36-hour stay beginning on a Tuesday morning crosses one midnight; the same 36 hours beginning on a Tuesday evening crosses two. Establish the actual timestamps — presentation, inpatient order, discharge — before any interpretive argument, because if the stay did cross two midnights the plan's factual premise is wrong and the interpretive argument may be unnecessary.

Note also that the benchmark permits time receiving hospital care before the inpatient order to count toward the expectation. Which period a commercial plan counts is a question for its own policy.

Serial troponins, telemetry and stress testing describe the resources anticipated. Listing them supports the account of expected duration; it does not by itself establish a status.

Three provisions, not one rule

Separate these before writing anything. Conflating them is the fastest way to have an otherwise sound appeal dismissed on the wrong ground.

What each provision is, and who it reaches
ProvisionWhat it doesReach
The benchmarkInpatient status is generally appropriate where the admitting physician reasonably expects, at admission, that the patient will require hospital care spanning at least two midnights.Medicare fee-for-service. MA organisations are required to apply Medicare inpatient admission criteria under CMS-4201-F.
The case-by-case exceptionPermits inpatient status where a two-midnight stay was not expected, on documented physician judgement about the specific patient.Same as the benchmark. Frequently forgotten — it is the correct route for a genuinely short expected stay, and it is not a weaker argument.
The medical review presumptionA procedural presumption governing how certain stays are selected for review.Fee-for-service only. It does not transfer to Medicare Advantage and it does not transfer to commercial plans. Invoking it outside FFS is a straightforward error.

Where this framework does not reach

Commercial and self-funded plans are not bound by the Medicare framework. Some incorporate a two-midnight benchmark into their own medical policy, and some cite it in denial rationales without having adopted it. Neither fact makes CMS the governing authority over that claim.

Where a commercial plan has cited the standard, the useful move is to hold the plan to the framework it chose — while sourcing the argument to the plan's own policy rather than to CMS. Where it has not, the Medicare material is background at best and a distraction at worst. That situation is what the companion article covers.

Why payers use retrospective reasoning anyway

Payer retrospective review applies hindsight bias systematically. The reviewer knows the final diagnosis. They know the patient was discharged in 36 hours. They know the stress test was negative. From that vantage point, the admission "looks like" observation. But the Two-Midnight standard explicitly prohibits this reasoning. The question is what the physician knew and reasonably expected at the time of admission — not what the chart says after the fact.

Payers continue to apply retrospective reasoning because it works. It works because most appeals do not cite the Two-Midnight standard. They describe the patient's clinical severity instead. "The patient was acutely ill and required inpatient monitoring" is true but insufficient. The reviewer's response is: "I agree the patient was ill. I disagree that the illness required inpatient-level care for two midnights."

What the appeal must do instead

The appeal must establish three things in sequence. First, it must frame the legal standard: CMS requires that inpatient status be evaluated based on the physician's reasonable expectation at the time of admission. Second, it must map the clinical evidence available at admission to that standard — showing what the physician knew, what the differential diagnosis included, what workup was planned, and why that workup reasonably required an inpatient stay spanning two midnights. Third, it must address the shorter-than-expected length of stay as evidence of effective inpatient management — not as evidence against the admission.

A patient who recovers faster than expected does not retroactively become an observation patient. A negative workup result does not retroactively prove the workup was unnecessary. These are fundamental Two-Midnight Rule principles, and they are frequently missing from the appeals this reviewer has seen. (See a full Two-Midnight defense memo →)

The documentation gap that costs hospitals the most

The single most common documentation gap in observation-downgrade appeals is the absence of a physician attestation documenting the clinical reasoning at the time of admission. Many admitting physicians write order sets that specify "admit inpatient" without documenting why they expected a two-midnight stay. The clinical reasoning exists — it is implicit in the workup plan, the specialist consultations ordered, and the monitoring protocols initiated. But it is not documented in the specific format the Two-Midnight standard evaluates.

A short physician attestation — like the documentation supplement recommended in the observation defense specimen — written at the time of admission or supplemented before the appeal — that connects the clinical presentation to the expected length of stay is often the difference between a successful and unsuccessful observation-downgrade appeal. This is not a retrospective justification. It is a contemporaneous documentation of the clinical judgment that the Two-Midnight standard evaluates.
